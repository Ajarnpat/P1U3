Principle of conservation of energy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can be transferred between energy stores/types and between a system and its surroundings. For a suitably chosen system, the total energy before an event equals the total energy after the event.
Total energy before = Total energy after

In AP Physics 1 mechanics, our quantitative energy toolkit is mainly:

K = ½mv²

Translational kinetic energy

ΔUg = mgΔy

Change in gravitational potential energy when g is approximately constant

Ug = −Gm₁m₂/r

General gravitational potential energy

Us = ½k(Δx)²

Ideal spring potential energy

ΔUint

Change in internal energy used as bookkeeping when mechanical energy is dissipated

Not in this lesson: we are deliberately not using the work-energy theorem or the mechanical-work equation. Today we solve by comparing energy before and energy after.

Throughout this lesson use g = 9.8 m s⁻².

Task 1

Identify the main energy transfer

For each event, choose the main starting and finishing energy type. The dropdowns deliberately contain all nine GREENSICK forms.

1. A small, dense ball falls through the air.
→
Reveal explanation

Gravitational → Kinetic. For a small dense ball, air resistance is small enough that the main transfer is gravitational potential energy to kinetic energy.

2. A car with its engine switched off coasts uphill and slows down.
→
Reveal explanation

Kinetic → Gravitational. The car loses kinetic energy while the car-Earth system gains gravitational potential energy. Small losses to internal energy are ignored in this main-transfer model.

3. The school lift descends at steady speed.
→
Reveal explanation

Gravitational → Internal. Its speed is constant, so its kinetic energy is not changing. The decrease in gravitational potential energy is transferred mainly to internal energy in the lift/motor/braking system.

4. A compressed spring launches a cart on a horizontal track.
→
Reveal explanation

Elastic → Kinetic. Spring potential energy decreases while the cart gains kinetic energy.

5. A bicycle brakes to rest on level ground.
→
Reveal explanation

Kinetic → Internal. The bicycle-rider system loses kinetic energy and the brakes/tyres/surroundings gain internal energy.

6. A bouncy ball strikes the floor and compresses before rebounding.
→
Reveal explanation

Kinetic → Elastic. During the compression phase, much of the ball’s kinetic energy is temporarily stored as elastic potential energy. Some also becomes internal energy and sound.

Task 2

Simple use of conservation of energy

A. Lightbulb

A 100 W bulb converts 100 J of electrical energy each second. If 75 J becomes internal energy each second, what other energy change can be deduced?

Hint

Account for all 100 J.

Worked answer

100 J − 75 J = 25 J. The remaining 25 J per second is transferred mainly as radiation (light).

B. Falling ball

A ball loses 250 J of gravitational potential energy and gains 200 J of kinetic energy. Suggest what else has occurred.

Hint

There is a 50 J difference between the mechanical energy lost and gained.

Worked answer

50 J has been transferred to other forms, principally internal energy (and possibly some sound) because of dissipative effects such as air resistance.

C. Elastic band

A stretched elastic band is released vertically upward. At the highest point the object has gained 25 J of gravitational potential energy. Neglect energy losses. What can you deduce about the elastic potential energy stored initially?

Worked answer

At the highest point the speed is zero. With no losses, the initial elastic potential energy must have been 25 J.

Task 3

Problem solving with gravitational and kinetic energy

A 28 g ball falls from rest from a table 78 cm high. Use SI units and give final numerical answers to 2 significant figures.

  1. Determine Δy with the correct sign.
  2. Determine ΔUg with the correct sign.
  3. Explain what that sign means physically.
  4. Use a kinematics equation to determine the speed just before impact.
  5. Determine the kinetic energy just before impact.
  6. Has the ball gained or lost kinetic energy?
Hints

Convert 28 g to kg and 78 cm to m. Take upward as positive, so Δy is negative. For the kinematics step use v² = v₀² + 2aΔy with a and Δy chosen consistently.

Worked solution

m = 0.028 kg, Δy = −0.78 m.

ΔUg = mgΔy = (0.028)(9.8)(−0.78) = −0.21 J. The negative sign means the gravitational potential energy of the ball-Earth system decreases.

From kinematics, v = √(2gh) = 3.9 m s⁻¹. Then K = ½mv² = 0.21 J. The ball gains 0.21 J of kinetic energy, matching the magnitude of the gravitational potential energy lost when air resistance is neglected.

Task 4

The before-and-after method

For each problem in the rest of this lesson, start by writing an energy statement. A useful template is:

Ki + Ug,i + Us,i + Uint,i = Kf + Ug,f + Us,f + Uint,f

Cross out terms that are zero or unchanged. This is an accounting method, not a new equation to memorize.

Quick check: A ball is dropped from rest with negligible drag. Which terms change?
Task 5

Practical: a bouncing ball

Equipment

Bouncy ball, mass balance, metre stick, stopwatch.

Method

  1. Measure the ball mass m.
  2. Choose and measure a drop height h₁.
  3. Release the ball from rest without pushing it.
  4. Estimate the maximum first rebound height h₂. Repeat at least five times and average.
  5. Optional cross-check: time between the first and second floor impacts. If the rebound is approximately vertical, h₂ ≈ g(Δt)²/8. Human reaction time makes this a rough measurement.

Quantify the energy

Ebefore ≈ mgh₁
Erebound ≈ mgh₂
ΔUint + Esound ≈ mg(h₁ − h₂)

Calculate the percentage of the original mechanical energy recovered on the rebound:

percentage recovered ≈ 100 × h₂/h₁
Discussion prompts
  • Where does the missing mechanical energy go?
  • Why is the result not exactly reproducible?
  • Which measurement contributes most uncertainty?
  • Would a heavier ball of the same material necessarily have a different percentage recovery?
Task 6

Compare Force & Motion with Energy

A. Block on a frictionless incline

A 1.2 kg block starts from rest. It moves 0.50 m down a frictionless incline while dropping vertically by 0.30 m.

  1. Determine the incline angle.
  2. Determine the component of weight parallel to the plane.
  3. Determine the acceleration.
  4. Use kinematics to determine the speed after moving 0.50 m.
  5. Determine ΔUg.
  6. Determine K at the same instant.
  7. Compare the energy change with the force-and-motion result.
Worked solution

sinθ = 0.30/0.50 = 0.60, so θ = 36.9°. F∥ = mg sinθ = 7.1 N. a = F/m = 5.9 m s⁻². v = √(2as) = 2.4 m s⁻¹. ΔUg = mgΔy = −3.5 J. K = ½mv² = 3.5 J. The decrease in Ug matches the increase in K.

B. Projectile launched from a building

A 0.25 kg ball is launched at 15 m s⁻¹, 30° above horizontal, from a building 12 m high. Air resistance is negligible. Define “initial” as just after launch and “final” first as the highest point.

  1. Find the initial horizontal and vertical velocity components.
  2. Use kinematics to find the rise above the roof.
  3. Find the initial K.
  4. At the top, find the horizontal velocity, speed and K.
  5. Find ΔK and ΔUg from launch to the highest point.
  6. Find ΔUg from the roof to the ground.
  7. Use conservation of energy to find the speed just before impact.
Worked solution

vx,0 = 13.0 m s⁻¹, vy,0 = 7.5 m s⁻¹. The rise is 2.87 m. Ki = 28.1 J. At the top, v = vx = 13.0 m s⁻¹, so Ktop = 21.1 J. Therefore ΔK = −7.03 J and ΔUg = +7.03 J. From roof to ground, ΔUg = −29.4 J, so K increases by 29.4 J to 57.5 J. Impact speed = 21.5 m s⁻¹.

Task 7

Explore gravitational potential energy further

Near Earth’s surface we usually use ΔUg = mgΔy. Over very large distances, g changes significantly, so use the general gravitational potential energy:

Ug = −Gm₁m₂/r

The zero is defined at infinite separation. A bound gravitational system therefore has negative Ug.

Take Earth’s mass as 5.98 × 10²⁴ kg, Earth’s radius as 6380 km, and your mass as 65 kg.

  1. Find Ug at Earth’s surface.
  2. Find Ug at 2 Earth radii from Earth’s centre.
  3. Find the increase in Ug.
  4. Find the energy required to escape to infinity.
  5. Now incorrectly assume g stays 9.8 m s⁻² while you rise by 6380 km. Calculate ΔUg = mgΔy and comment on the validity.
Worked solution

At r = RE, Ug ≈ −4.1 × 10⁹ J. At r = 2RE, Ug ≈ −2.0 × 10⁹ J. The increase is ≈ +2.0 × 10⁹ J. Escape to infinity requires ≈ 4.1 × 10⁹ J. Using constant g over 6380 km gives ≈ 4.1 × 10⁹ J for the rise to 2RE, roughly twice the correct increase, because g becomes much smaller with altitude.

Task 8

Dropping a ball with drag

A 520 g ball falls from rest through 14 m.

  1. Find the magnitude of the decrease in gravitational potential energy.
  2. If all of it becomes kinetic energy, find K and the speed.
  3. Instead suppose half of the lost gravitational potential energy becomes internal energy. Find the final speed.
  4. Assume acceleration was constant. Find the acceleration.
  5. Use force analysis to find the air-resistance force.
Worked solution

Loss of Ug = 71 J. Ideal K = 71 J, giving v = 17 m s⁻¹. If half becomes internal energy, K = 35.7 J and v = 12 m s⁻¹. Constant-acceleration kinematics gives a = 4.9 m s⁻² downward. Weight is 5.10 N downward and net force is 2.55 N downward, so air resistance is 2.55 N upward.

Task 9

Generalise by deriving an equation

An object of mass M starts from rest and falls through vertical height H. Ignore air resistance. Use conservation of energy to derive its final speed.

Worked derivation

MgH = ½Mv². Mass cancels, so v = √(2gH).

Now the same object begins with downward speed v0. Derive its speed after falling through H.

Worked derivation

½Mv0² + MgH = ½Mv². Therefore v² = v0² + 2gH, so v = √(v0² + 2gH). This matches the familiar constant-acceleration kinematics relationship.

Task 10

Projectiles using conservation of energy

1. Horizontal launch

A 2.5 kg object is projected horizontally at 5.0 m s⁻¹ from a building 12 m high. Ignore air resistance.

  1. Find the initial K.
  2. Find the decrease in Ug and hence the increase in K.
  3. Find the final K and the impact speed.
Worked solution

Ki = 31 J. Decrease in Ug has magnitude 294 J, so Kf = 325 J. Impact speed = 16 m s⁻¹.

2. Generalise

For mass M, initial horizontal speed v0, and building height H, derive the impact speed.

Worked derivation

½Mv0² + MgH = ½Mv² → v = √(v0² + 2gH).

3. Angled projectile

A 0.500 kg object is projected from ground level at 12 m s⁻¹, 60° above horizontal.

  1. Find initial K.
  2. Find the horizontal velocity component.
  3. State how that component changes in flight.
  4. At the highest point, state the vertical velocity and find K.
  5. Find the kinetic energy lost and gravitational potential energy gained.
  6. Find the maximum vertical height.
Worked solution

Ki = 36 J. vx = 6.0 m s⁻¹ and remains constant. At the top vy = 0, so K = 9.0 J. The object loses 27 J of K, so it gains 27 J of Ug. Hence h = 27/(0.500×9.8) = 5.5 m.

Task 11

Inclined planes: dynamics or energy?

A 2.0 kg block starts from rest on a frictionless 30° incline and moves 0.40 m down the slope.

A. Force & motion

  1. Find the component of weight down the slope.
  2. Find acceleration.
  3. Find final speed.

B. Conservation of energy

  1. Find the vertical drop h.
  2. Find the decrease in Ug and final K.
  3. Find final speed.
Worked solution

Force method: Fnet = mg sin30° = 9.8 N, a = 4.9 m s⁻², v = 2.0 m s⁻¹. Energy method: h = 0.40 sin30° = 0.20 m, gravitational potential energy decreases by 3.92 J, so Kf = 3.92 J and v = 2.0 m s⁻¹.

C. Generalise

For mass M, incline angle θ and distance d down a frictionless plane, derive h, the gravitational-energy decrease, and final speed.

Worked derivation

h = d sinθ. The decrease in gravitational potential energy has magnitude Mg d sinθ. Therefore ½Mv² = Mg d sinθ, so v = √(2gd sinθ). Mass cancels; this is consistent with the acceleration of a frictionless block being independent of mass.

Task 12

Challenge algebra problem: rough inclined plane

A block of mass m starts from rest and slides down a rough plane inclined at θ. Its vertical height drop is h and the coefficient of kinetic friction is μ. The acceleration is down the plane. Derive expressions using only m, θ, h, μ and g.

  1. Distance d moved along the plane.
  2. Kinetic-friction force.
  3. Net force down the plane.
  4. Acceleration.
  5. Final speed.
  6. Final kinetic energy.
  7. Change in gravitational potential energy ΔUg.
  8. Increase in internal energy ΔUint, using conservation of energy.
Hints

Use d = h/sinθ. The normal force is mg cosθ. For the energy step, choose Ug,f = 0 and write initial gravitational potential energy = final K + increase in internal energy.

Worked derivation

(a) d = h/sinθ.

(b) Ff = μmg cosθ.

(c) Fnet = mg(sinθ − μcosθ).

(d) a = g(sinθ − μcosθ).

(e) v = √[2gh(1 − μcotθ)].

(f) Kf = mgh(1 − μcotθ).

(g) ΔUg = −mgh.

(h) Conservation gives mgh = Kf + ΔUint, so ΔUint = μmgh cotθ.
